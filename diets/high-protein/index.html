High-Protein Diet

Protein-first eating for strength athletes and anyone who wants to stay full.

A high-protein diet sets protein near 2 g per kg of bodyweight. Protein supports muscle repair and is the most filling macronutrient.

Spread protein across 3-5 meals of roughly 25-45 g each. People with kidney disease should consult a doctor before raising protein intake.

Who this diet is for

A high-protein diet suits strength athletes, people cutting body fat, older adults trying to keep muscle, and anyone who struggles with hunger. It can be combined with any calorie target.

Why it works

Protein supports muscle repair and growth, and higher intakes help preserve muscle during a deficit. It also has the strongest effect on fullness of the three macronutrients and a higher thermic effect, meaning more of its calories are used in digestion.

In a one-year study, resistance-trained men eating very high protein intakes showed no harmful effects on kidney or liver markers. People with existing kidney disease are a different case and need medical advice.

Calories and macros

Protein2.2 g per kg of bodyweight (1.0 g per lb)
Fat27% of calories
CarbohydratesThe remaining calories after protein and fat

What to eat

  • Chicken, turkey, lean beef and fish
  • Eggs, Greek yogurt, skyr and cottage cheese
  • Whey or plant protein when needed

What to limit

  • Meals without a protein source

How to start

  1. Set protein at about 2.0-2.2 g per kg of bodyweight.
  2. Spread it over 3-5 meals of roughly 0.4 g per kg each, usually 30-45 g.
  3. Build every meal around a protein food, then add carbohydrates, fats and vegetables.
  4. Use lean sources when calories are limited and fattier sources when bulking.
  5. Drink enough water and keep fiber up with vegetables, fruit and whole grains.

Common mistakes

Squeezing out carbohydrates

Very high protein with very low carbohydrate can reduce training performance. Protein should be high, not the only focus.

Relying on shakes

Whole foods bring micronutrients and fiber. Use powder to fill gaps, not as most of your intake.

Ignoring fiber

Meat-heavy, low-plant diets often cause digestive issues.

Frequently asked questions

Is too much protein bad for the kidneys?

Research in healthy people has not found harm at intakes common among lifters. People with kidney disease should follow medical advice.

Can I absorb more than 30 g of protein in one meal?

Yes. The body absorbs larger amounts; the idea of a strict 30 g limit is a myth, although spreading protein through the day is still sensible.
